Custom Automatic Spray Guns: 7 Things to Know Before You Customize

Mar 05, 2026

A practical, engineering-focused guide for B2B manufacturers evaluating custom automatic spray guns, covering fluid compatibility, transfer efficiency, robotics/PLC integration, wear materials, atomization control, maintenance design, and total cost of ownership.

Custom Automatic Spray Guns: 7 Things to Know Before You Customize

For many B2B manufacturing facilities, standard automatic spray guns work well enough for basic jobs. But once the coating process becomes more demanding, off-the-shelf equipment often starts to show its limits. That usually happens when production lines deal with abrasive materials, strict environmental requirements, or high-speed robotic systems.

When that mismatch happens, the cost shows up quickly. Plants may see more overspray, uneven finishes, unplanned maintenance, higher material consumption, and more pressure to control VOC emissions.

That is why more manufacturers are looking at custom automatic spray solutions. A spray gun that is designed around a specific coating, line speed, robot setup, and maintenance routine can help improve consistency and reduce waste. At the same time, customization is not something to approach casually. Procurement teams and project engineers need to evaluate the technical requirements and business case carefully before moving forward.

Here are seven important things to review before customizing an automatic spray gun.

Why Custom Spray Solutions Matter More in Modern Manufacturing

As automated finishing systems become faster and more precise, the spray equipment connected to them also needs to become more specialized. Market data suggests the global spray painting robot market is growing at a CAGR of more than 10.6%. That growth reflects a broader shift toward more advanced, connected production environments.

In practice, this means spray guns are no longer just simple application tools. They are part of a larger automated system that includes robots, PLCs, sensors, and quality controls. For high-volume manufacturers, custom automatic spray guns are increasingly becoming a practical requirement rather than a niche upgrade.

1. Fluid Viscosity and Coating Compatibility

The first thing to understand is the coating material itself. Fluid viscosity has a major impact on how a spray gun should be designed internally.

Some coatings behave like Newtonian fluids, meaning their viscosity stays fairly constant. Others are non-Newtonian, meaning viscosity changes under shear. If a facility is spraying thick adhesives, anti-corrosive primers, or abrasive ceramic coatings, standard fluid passages may clog or wear out too quickly.

A custom spray gun can be designed with fluid passages sized for the actual material being used. It can also include air cap configurations that support proper atomization without damaging the coating or disrupting flow. If the process involves waterborne coatings, stainless steel wetted parts are often important to reduce the risk of internal corrosion and contamination.

2. Transfer Efficiency and Environmental Compliance

Transfer Efficiency, or TE, refers to how much coating reaches the target surface compared with how much is lost as overspray. In high-volume production, even a relatively small drop in TE can translate into major material loss over time.

This is one of the main reasons customization matters. Before selecting a spray gun design, teams need to decide whether HVLP, LVMP, or conventional air spray technology best fits their performance and compliance goals.

HVLP systems, for example, are often associated with transfer efficiency in the 65% to 70% range, depending on the application and setup. That can help reduce overspray and support compliance with EPA and local VOC regulations. The right choice depends on the coating, finish requirement, production speed, and local environmental rules.

3. Integration with Robots and PLC Systems

An automatic spray gun is usually part of a much larger automation setup. It may sit on the end of a robot arm or work within a reciprocating system, so the gun has to fit both the mechanical and control requirements of that environment.

Before customizing, manufacturers need to look closely at robotic payload limits, arm speed, and motion patterns. A lighter gun body, such as one made from forged aluminum, may help reduce inertia and support faster directional changes.

Control integration also matters. Some applications need fast-response solenoid valves that can trigger fluid flow within milliseconds. That level of response becomes especially important on high-speed lines, where coating needs to start and stop precisely to avoid waste and maintain finish quality.

4. Nozzle and Needle Material Selection

Wear resistance is a critical issue, especially when spraying abrasive coatings. Over time, friction from atomized fluid can erode nozzles and needles, which changes the spray pattern and affects finish consistency.

For standard applications, common stainless steel materials may be adequate. But for zinc-rich primers, glass-flake coatings, and other abrasive materials, harder materials such as Tungsten Carbide or hardened SS316 are often worth considering.

Tungsten Carbide usually comes with a higher upfront cost, but it may significantly extend component life in demanding applications. Actual service life will depend on the coating type, abrasiveness, operating pressure, and maintenance practices, so it is better to treat lifespan improvements as application-dependent rather than universal.

5. Spray Pattern and Atomization Control

Different parts need different spray patterns. A wide fan may work well for flat panels, while a narrower pattern may be better for internal surfaces or cylindrical geometries.

This is where atomization precision becomes important. Droplet size, often discussed in terms such as Sauter Mean Diameter or SMD, has a direct effect on finish quality and coating behavior. Custom air caps can be designed to produce a pattern that matches the geometry of the target part more closely.

It is also useful when the system allows separate control of fan air and atomization air. That gives engineers more flexibility to fine-tune the process based on finish requirements, fluid behavior, and operating speed.

6. Maintenance Access and Downtime Reduction

In continuous production environments, maintenance design is not a small detail. The easier a spray gun is to clean, inspect, and service, the less disruption it creates on the line.

Custom spray guns can be designed with features that simplify routine maintenance. One example is a quick-release manifold baseplate, which can allow technicians to remove the gun head without disconnecting multiple air and fluid lines. That can make cleaning and replacement much faster.

Design simplicity also matters. Fewer seals and O-rings can mean fewer leak points, fewer wear parts, and shorter rebuild times. For plants running 24/7, these details can have a meaningful impact on uptime.

7. Total Cost of Ownership, Not Just Purchase Price

A custom automatic spray gun will usually cost more upfront than a standard model, and it may also require a longer lead time. But the better way to evaluate it is through Total Cost of Ownership, or TCO.

The real return often comes from a combination of factors. That may include better transfer efficiency, lower paint consumption, reduced maintenance time, fewer rejected parts, and less rework. In the right application, those savings can outweigh the higher purchase cost within a relatively short period.

For procurement teams, the key is to compare the full operating picture rather than focusing only on the initial purchase order.

In general, standard spray guns remain a practical choice for simpler coating tasks. But once the process involves specialized fluids, high throughput, or robotic integration, custom designs tend to offer more control and better long-term economics.

Industry Trends Shaping Automated Spraying

Several broader industry trends are influencing how manufacturers think about spray gun customization.

AI and Sensor-Based Monitoring

Newer systems are increasingly being paired with IoT sensors that monitor pressure, flow, and wear in real time. That data can feed predictive maintenance programs and help identify performance issues before they cause downtime.

More Robotic Spraying

As robotic coating systems become more common, spray guns need to match the physical and control demands of those systems. Automated and robotic spray guns now represent a substantial share of new installations in heavy equipment manufacturing, and lightweight customization is becoming more important.

Stronger Sustainability Pressure

Environmental compliance continues to push manufacturers toward higher transfer efficiency and lower emissions. That is one reason why HVLP and electrostatic configurations are getting more attention, especially in facilities trying to reduce overspray and VOC output.

Executive Takeaways

For procurement managers, engineers, and operations leaders, the decision to customize an automatic spray gun should start with process requirements, not product catalogs.

A useful evaluation framework includes the following:

  • Fluid analysis: Review viscosity, abrasiveness, and chemical compatibility.

  • Efficiency targets: Define TE goals and environmental compliance needs.

  • Automation fit: Confirm payload, dimensions, valve response, and PLC integration.

  • Maintenance design: Look for features that reduce cleaning and replacement time.

  • TCO model: Compare long-term operating savings against engineering and lead-time costs.

Automatic Spray Gun Buyer FAQ

How long does a custom automatic spray gun project usually take?

For many projects, engineering, prototyping, and production may take around 4 to 8 weeks. Actual lead time will vary depending on design complexity, materials, and testing requirements.

Can one custom spray gun handle both waterborne and solvent-based coatings?

In many cases, yes. That depends on selecting suitable wetted materials, such as higher-grade stainless steel, and confirming compatibility with the specific coatings involved.

How should ROI be calculated?

A practical ROI model usually includes three categories: paint savings from improved transfer efficiency, labor and downtime savings from easier maintenance, and reduced rework or rejection caused by more consistent atomization.

Conclusion

Custom automatic spray guns are not the right answer for every coating line, but they can make a meaningful difference when the application is complex, high-volume, or tightly integrated with robotics. The most important factors usually come down to fluid behavior, transfer efficiency, integration requirements, wear resistance, maintenance access, and total cost of ownership. When those variables are reviewed carefully, customization becomes easier to justify as an operational decision rather than just a capital expense.

Why EV Wire Harnesses Need Different Protection Methods

Jul 07, 2026

Learn why EV wire harnesses require different protection for power circuits, signal lines, and harsh environments, including insulation, shielding, routing, and inspection.

Why EV Wire Harnesses Need Different Protection for Power, Signal and Harsh Environments

Electric vehicles place far greater demands on wire harness design than many conventional low-voltage automotive systems. In an EV, wiring is not only responsible for basic electrical connection; it also supports power delivery, battery management, sensor communication, control signals, charging interfaces, and safety-related functions. As the number of electronic systems increases, the wire harness becomes a critical structure that must manage electrical performance, mechanical durability, thermal exposure, and signal integrity at the same time.

This is why EV wire harnesses cannot be protected with a single, uniform approach. A cable carrying high-voltage power has different risks from a sensor signal line. A harness routed near a battery pack, motor controller, wheel area, or underbody structure faces different conditions from one placed inside a protected cabin module. Standards and regulations also reflect this difference. For example, U.S. FMVSS 305 defines high-voltage sources as components with working voltage greater than 60 VDC or 30 VAC and requires high-voltage cables outside electrical protection barriers to have orange outer covering. ISO 6469-3 also addresses electrical safety requirements for voltage class B circuits in electrically propelled road vehicles.

Understanding these differences helps engineering, purchasing, and production teams specify EV wire harnesses more realistically.

Power Circuits Need Protection Against Voltage, Current, and Heat

Power harnesses in EVs are designed to transfer energy between battery packs, inverters, motor controllers, DC/DC converters, on-board chargers, and related high-current systems. Their primary design concerns are electrical insulation, conductor sizing, contact resistance, heat buildup, and protection against direct contact.

Unlike ordinary low-voltage wiring, high-voltage EV cables must maintain safe insulation under operating stress, vibration, temperature changes, and long service life. Cable selection may involve voltage rating, conductor material, insulation thickness, shielding structure, bending radius, and terminal compatibility. The ISO 19642 series covers automotive cable vocabulary, test methods, and requirements, including cable types for 600 V AC / 900 V DC and 1,000 V AC / 1,500 V DC road vehicle applications.

Power harness protection also depends heavily on connection quality. A poor crimp, loose terminal, or unsuitable connector can increase resistance and generate heat. In EV systems, this is not only a performance issue but also a reliability and safety concern. Therefore, terminal crimping, pull-force testing, continuity testing, and assembly verification are commonly used to reduce production risk.

Signal Circuits Need Protection Against Noise and Interference

Signal harnesses are different. Their main job is not to carry large amounts of power, but to transmit accurate information between sensors, control units, battery management systems, displays, and vehicle electronics. The key challenge is signal stability.

EV platforms include many possible sources of electromagnetic noise, including inverters, motor drives, charging systems, switching power electronics, and high-current cables. If signal wires are routed too close to noisy power cables, or if grounding and shielding are not carefully considered, the system may experience unstable readings, communication errors, or intermittent faults.

This is why signal harness protection often focuses on shielding, twisted pair construction, grounding strategy, connector quality, and routing separation. Vehicle-level electromagnetic compatibility is also a recognized regulatory issue. UNECE Regulation No. 10 applies to vehicles with regard to electromagnetic compatibility, while also covering electrical and electronic sub-assemblies in vehicle approval contexts.

In practice, this means power and signal harnesses should not simply be bundled together for convenience. Engineers often separate high-current routes from sensitive communication lines, use shielding where needed, and define clear routing paths during the design stage.

Harsh-Environment Harnesses Need Mechanical and Environmental Protection

Some EV harnesses are installed in relatively protected areas, such as dashboard modules or interior control boxes. Others are exposed to vibration, abrasion, temperature variation, moisture, road debris, and maintenance handling. These harsh-environment harnesses need additional mechanical and environmental protection.

Common protective measures include abrasion-resistant sleeving, corrugated tubing, heat-resistant covering, waterproof connectors, strain relief, grommets, fixing clips, and customized bundling. These choices depend on where the harness is installed. A harness routed near a motor controller may need more attention to heat and electromagnetic noise. A harness installed near the chassis or underbody may need stronger resistance against water, stone impact, bending, and vibration. A harness used in an electric forklift or delivery vehicle may require durability against frequent operation and maintenance cycles.

Manufacturing workmanship is also part of protection. IPC/WHMA-A-620 is widely recognized as an industry-consensus standard for requirements and acceptance of cable and wire harness assemblies. While project-specific automotive requirements may vary, documented assembly quality, inspection criteria, and repeatable processes are essential for reducing field issues.

Harness Type Main Risk Typical Protection Focus
High-voltage power harness Electric shock, heat, insulation failure, poor contact Voltage-rated cable, orange identification, insulation, secure terminals, continuity and pull-force testing
Signal harness EMI, unstable data, communication error Shielding, twisted pair design, routing separation, grounding control, connector reliability
Harsh-environment harness Abrasion, vibration, moisture, temperature change Protective sleeving, sealing, strain relief, clips, grommets, environmental inspection

Why One Protection Method Is Not Enough

A common mistake in EV harness planning is to treat protection as a general accessory rather than a design decision. Adding tubing or tape may improve abrasion resistance, but it does not solve electromagnetic interference. Shielding may help signal stability, but it does not automatically improve water resistance. A larger wire gauge may support current flow, but it does not guarantee correct terminal crimping or connector retention.

Each harness should be evaluated according to electrical load, signal sensitivity, routing position, installation space, service conditions, and inspection requirements. This is especially important for customized EV platforms, electric buses, electric delivery vehicles, electric forklifts, and specialty electric vehicles, where the layout may differ significantly from standard passenger cars.

A well-designed EV harness is not only about selecting the right wire. It is about matching the wire, terminal, connector, protection material, routing method, and inspection process to the actual application.

FAQ

1. Why are many EV high-voltage cables orange?

Orange is used as a safety identification color for high-voltage cables in many EV applications. Under FMVSS 305, high-voltage cables located outside electrical protection barriers must be identified with orange outer covering. This helps technicians and emergency responders recognize high-voltage areas more easily.

2. Do all EV wire harnesses need shielding?

No. Shielding is mainly used when electromagnetic interference may affect signal quality or when cables may generate interference that affects nearby electronics. Some power cables and sensitive signal cables may require shielding, but other low-risk circuits may not.

3. Why should power and signal wires be routed separately?

Power cables can carry high current and may generate electromagnetic noise, especially near inverters and motor controllers. Separating signal wires from high-current routes helps reduce interference and improves communication stability.

4. What protection is needed for underbody EV harnesses?

Underbody harnesses may need abrasion resistance, waterproofing, fixing clips, grommets, strain relief, and protection against vibration or stone impact. The exact design depends on vehicle layout and operating conditions.

5. Is wire gauge the only important factor in EV power harness design?

No. Wire gauge is important for current capacity, but insulation rating, terminal crimping, connector compatibility, heat management, routing, and inspection are also critical.

6. Why is quality inspection important for EV cable assemblies?

Inspection helps identify problems such as poor crimping, unstable continuity, incorrect wire length, weak terminal retention, or assembly mismatch. These checks reduce risks during installation, vehicle operation, and maintenance.

Conclusion

EV wire harness protection must be application-specific. Power circuits require attention to voltage, current, heat, insulation, and safe identification. Signal circuits require stable communication and protection against electromagnetic interference. Harnesses installed in harsh environments require mechanical, thermal, and environmental durability.

As EV platforms become more complex, wire harness design should be considered early in the development process rather than treated as a final assembly detail. When protection methods are selected according to real electrical and environmental requirements, manufacturers can reduce production issues, improve service reliability, and support safer long-term vehicle operation.
